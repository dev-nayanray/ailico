## What UTM parameters are

UTM parameters are five standard query tags you append to a URL. When someone
clicks the tagged link, analytics platforms such as Google Analytics, Matomo, and
Plausible read the tags and attribute the visit to a specific campaign — so your
reports show *which* effort produced the visitor, instead of lumping everything
into "direct".

The five parameters:

| Parameter | Required? | What it answers | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `utm_source` | Yes | Where is the link published? | `newsletter`, `google`, `linkedin` |
| `utm_medium` | Yes | What kind of marketing is it? | `email`, `cpc`, `social`, `referral` |
| `utm_campaign` | Yes | Which specific promotion? | `spring-sale-2026`, `launch` |
| `utm_term` | No | Which keyword (paid search)? | `running+shoes` |
| `utm_content` | No | Which creative or variant? | `banner-a`, `cta-top` |

Source + medium + campaign is the minimum for usable reports. Term matters mainly
for paid search; content is how you compare creative variants against each other.

## How to build a UTM link correctly

1. **Start with the destination URL** — the page you want people to land on, in
   full, including `https://`.
2. **Add the three required tags** with values that describe the placement, not
   the business. A link in your weekly newsletter is `utm_source=newsletter`,
   `utm_medium=email`, `utm_campaign=october-week-2`.
3. **URL-encode values** — spaces become `%20`. Any proper builder does this for
   you automatically (hand-editing invites typos).
4. **Test the finished link** before publishing: open it and confirm the page
   loads and the tags survive any redirect.
5. **Record it.** Keep a simple sheet of the links you create — your future self
   compiling the monthly report will thank you.

A finished link looks like this:

```text
https://example.com/spring-offer?utm_source=newsletter&utm_medium=email&utm_campaign=spring-sale-2026
```

## The naming conventions that keep reports clean

The single biggest UTM problem is not technical — it is inconsistency. Analytics
platforms treat values as case-sensitive exact strings, so these three links all
report as *different* sources:

```text
?utm_source=LinkedIn    ← capital L
?utm_source=linkedin    ← clean
?utm_source=li          ← abbreviation someone invented once
```

Adopt three rules before your first campaign and never change them:

1. **Everything lowercase.** `newsletter`, never `Newsletter`.
2. **No spaces.** Use hyphens: `spring-sale`, not `spring sale` (which encodes to
   the unreadable `spring%20sale`).
3. **One canonical name per source.** Write the list down: `newsletter`, `google`,
   `facebook`, `instagram`, `linkedin`, `youtube`, `twitter`. If a source is not
   on the list, add it to the list first, then use it.

A useful pattern for campaign names is `topic-variant` or `topic-month` —
`ai-tools-report-oct` is recognizable in a report six months later; `campaign2`
is not.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Tagging internal links.** UTMs on your own site's navigation overwrite the
  visitor's original source. Tag only links on *external* channels (email, social,
  ads, partners).
- **UTM on the homepage "just in case."** Send each campaign to the most specific
  relevant page. It makes reporting — and retargeting — far cleaner.
- **Renaming mid-campaign.** Changing `spring-sale` to `springsale` halfway
  through splits one campaign into two rows. Pick names before launch and stick
  with them.
- **Forgetting redirects.** If your link shortener or CMS strips query parameters
  on redirect, the tags are lost. Always test the final URL, post-redirect.

## Do UTM links affect SEO?

UTM parameters do not affect your page speed, and search engines generally handle
them, but there is one hygiene step worth taking: ensure your pages carry a
**canonical tag** pointing at the clean URL. Then any tagged variant of the
address (`/offer?utm_source=...`) consolidates to the canonical version in search
indexes, while your analytics still reads the campaign tags. If you use WordPress,
most SEO plugins emit canonicals automatically; our [Website Quick
Fix](/services/website-quick-fix) includes checking exactly this kind of metadata
issue.

## FAQ

<details>
<summary>Do I need to configure anything in Google Analytics for UTMs to work?</summary>

No. Universal properties and GA4 read standard UTM parameters automatically on
any incoming click. The only requirement is that the tags are present and
correctly spelled on the destination URL.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the difference between utm_source and utm_medium?</summary>

Source is the specific origin (google, newsletter, linkedin). Medium is the
channel category (cpc, email, social). One medium groups many sources: `social`
covers facebook, instagram, and linkedin. In reports, "source / medium" together
answer where the visitor came from and via what kind of marketing.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Are shortened links (bit.ly etc.) compatible with UTMs?</summary>

Yes — add the UTM parameters *before* shortening, and verify the shortener passes
query parameters through (the major ones do). Test the final short link: the
landing URL must still contain your tags after all redirects.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Can I see UTM data without Google Analytics?</summary>

Yes. Most privacy-focused analytics tools (Matomo, Plausible, Fathom) read UTM
tags natively. Even server logs show the query string, though you will be doing
more manual work there.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How many UTM links is too many?</summary>

There is no hard limit, but every distinct combination is a new row in reports.
Prefer a small set of well-named links over dozens of near-duplicates — another
reason to keep a canonical naming sheet.

</details>
